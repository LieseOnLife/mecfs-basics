# Summary
This six-page handout from the US ME/CFS Clinician Coalition, a group of about 20 US ME/CFS specialists, is a primary-care primer. Its full title is "Diagnosing and Treating Myalgic Encephalomyelitis/Chronic Fatigue Syndrome (ME/CFS)", Version 2, July 2020. It covers:
- how to recognize ME/CFS under the 2015 National Academy of Medicine (IOM) criteria;
- how to identify PEM;
- what to rule out and what commonly co-occurs;
- a five-step basic management approach.

The Coalition's Clinical Management page labels it "2020 ME/CFS Clinician Coalition Handout: Basics of Diagnosis and Treatment". As of 2026-11-06 it was the newest version on the site: the V2 PDF was uploaded in June 2021, and the site's media library had no later version.

# Methods
- **Design.** This is an expert-consensus handout from a named group of clinicians (members listed on page 1) and cites no evidence grading. It has no patient sample (`n` blank). Mary Dimmock and Lily Chu, MD, helped compile it.
- **Case definition.** Diagnosis follows the NAM/IOM 2015 criteria, so `case_definition: [IOM]` and `pem_required: true`. The handout says experts may also use the CCC (2003) or ICC (2011) to validate a diagnosis.
- **Dates.** "July 2020" is printed on every page; no day is given, so `published` uses the first of the month. The PDF was fetched on 2026-11-06 from the Coalition's Clinical Management page.
- **Disclosures.** The handout carries no funding or conflict-of-interest statement.

# Findings
- **Core symptoms.** The core features are:
  - reduced pre-illness activity for 6 months or more, with profound fatigue not relieved by rest;
  - PEM;
  - unrefreshing sleep;
  - cognitive impairment and/or orthostatic intolerance.

  These must be moderate to severe and present at least 50% of the time. The handout calls PEM the clinical hallmark and says it has been objectively associated with impaired aerobic energy metabolism and orthostatic stress.
- **Identifying PEM.** The handout gives four screening questions for PEM and suggests a two-week symptom and activity diary. Orthostatic intolerance can be assessed with a NASA lean test or a tilt-table test.
- **Testing.** No validated diagnostic test exists. Testing is for alternative or comorbid conditions.
- **Differential and comorbidities.** The handout gives an extensive differential-diagnosis table and a separate table of common comorbidities, including POTS and neurally mediated hypotension, mast cell activation syndrome, Ehlers-Danlos syndrome, fibromyalgia, small fiber neuropathy, and sleep disorders. It notes that the 2015 NAM report made ME/CFS a positive diagnosis that can coexist with other conditions.
- **Basic management, in five steps:**
  1. Validate the illness and educate.
  2. Address impaired function and support needs (accommodations, disability, assistive devices).
  3. Teach pacing, using activity logs and heart-rate or activity monitors, to prevent or minimize PEM.
  4. Treat symptoms.
  5. Treat comorbid conditions.
- **Examples of commonly used medicines:**
  - *Pain:* low-dose naltrexone, duloxetine, gabapentin, or pregabalin.
  - *Orthostatic intolerance:* fludrocortisone, midodrine, pyridostigmine, low-dose beta blockers, and IV hydration.
  - *Cognition or fatigue (occasional use):* modafinil, methylphenidate, or dexedrine.
  - *Sleep:* trazodone, clonazepam, tricyclic antidepressants, or suvorexant.
- **Prescribing.** Drugs should be started low and increased slowly because of drug sensitivity.
- **Non-drug measures.** Salt and fluid loading, compression stockings, cognitive aids, ear plugs, eye masks, sunglasses, sleep hygiene, avoiding problem foods, and meditation.
- **Beyond the basics.** Some experts are evaluating selective use of antivirals and immune modulators and have seen efficacy in some patients. No agents are named.
- **Basic facts cited:**
  - 836,000 to 2.5 million Americans affected, with 84–91% undiagnosed;
  - women affected three times as often as men;
  - at least 25% housebound or bedbound and up to 75% unable to work or attend school;
  - annual US cost of 17 to 24 billion dollars.

# Limitations / flags
- Bateman et al. (Mayo Clinic Proceedings, 2021) is a peer-reviewed Coalition article by largely the same clinicians that covers the same ground in more depth.
- This is a short expert handout, not a systematic review or graded guideline. The medicine list is illustrative and is described as not exhaustive. It says these treatments "can help reduce the severity of symptoms" but cites no trials for that; its references are primers, case-definition papers, a JAMA pathophysiology review, and clinician guides.
- `treatments` links every medicine and non-drug measure the handout names, except meditation and cognitive or sensory aids.

# Abstract
No abstract. The handout describes itself as basic information on recognizing the distinctive symptoms of ME/CFS, identifying PEM, differential diagnosis and comorbidities, and "treating and managing ME/CFS (the basics)" for medical providers.

# Full text


Source: *Diagnosing and Treating Myalgic Encephalomyelitis/Chronic Fatigue Syndrome (ME/CFS)*, Version 2, July 2020, pp. 2–6.

## Presentation, diagnosis and core symptoms

The onset of ME/CFS is often sudden. Frequently, patients report that an infectious-like syndrome or infectious disease (such as infectious mononucleosis or flu-like illness) preceded the onset of their disease. ^q1

Diagnosis requires a thorough medical history, a physical exam, and recognition of the following core symptoms: ^q2

- Substantial reduction or impairment in the ability to engage in pre-illness activity that persists for 6 months or more and is accompanied by fatigue. The fatigue is profound, not lifelong, not the result of ongoing exertion, and not alleviated by rest. ^q3
- Post-exertional malaise (PEM) in which physical or mental activities result in a typically delayed and prolonged exacerbation of symptoms and reduction in functioning (discussed further below). ^q4
- Unrefreshing sleep and a variety of sleep disturbances ^q5
- Either cognitive impairment (often referred to as “brain fog” by patients) and/or orthostatic intolerance (the development of symptoms when upright that are alleviated when lying down) ^q6

These core features of illness must be moderate to severe and present at least 50% of the time; this is key to separating ME/CFS from other common causes of chronic fatigue. PEM is the clinical hallmark of ME/CFS and its most distinctive symptom. ^q7

## Post-exertional malaise

Patients experiencing PEM will often describe a “crash,” “relapse,” or “collapse” after even small amounts of mental or physical exertion that was previously tolerated. During the crash, which may be immediate but more often delayed by hours or days, patients can experience an exacerbation of one or all of their symptoms and a further reduction in functioning. It can take hours, days, a week or even longer to return to their previous baseline after a crash. For some patients, even basic activities of daily living can result in PEM. ^q8

It may help patients and clinicians to identify PEM if patients keep a daily diary of their symptoms and activities for two weeks. ^q9

## Diagnostic testing and differential diagnosis

While research studies have demonstrated numerous differences in biological measurements in ME/CFS, there is not yet a test validated for the diagnosis of ME/CFS in clinical practice. But there are many diagnostic tests to help identify other fatiguing illnesses or comorbid conditions that are often found in people with ME/CFS. ^q10

Orthostatic intolerance can be assessed with a NASA Lean Test (standing test) or a tilt table test for 10-20 minutes or up to 30 minutes for neurally mediated hypotension. ^q11

In the past, a diagnosis of ME/CFS was made by first excluding all other possible conditions. However, the 2015 National Academy of Medicine report established ME/CFS as a positive diagnosis that can coexist with other conditions, including those in the differential diagnosis. Recognizing co-morbid conditions early and treating them appropriately may improve the patient's health, function, and quality of life. ^q12

## Treating and managing ME/CFS

Patients may have experienced skepticism about their disease. The most important thing that a medical provider can do is to validate the illness for the patient and the patient’s family. Explain that ME/CFS is a serious medical illness and is not laziness, depression, or a psychosomatic disorder. ^q13

By definition, patients with ME/CFS have significant disability. Patients may need help in obtaining accommodations for school and work or when applying for disability. They may also need parking permits, assistive devices, and/or home health aides. Because of the financial impact, some patients may need help with shelter and food. ^q14

An important role for the medical provider is to teach patients about PEM and how to manage it with pacing. Pacing is an individualized approach to managing physical, cognitive, and emotional energy within a patient’s specific limits by carefully planning where and how to spend their available energy. It is a critical tool to prevent and/or reduce PEM. Activity logs and heart rate and activity monitors can be used to help patients understand when they are overdoing their specific energy limits. Even with such aids, pacing is a challenging task and some setbacks are inevitable, especially since tolerance for activity can vary from patient to patient and day to day. ^q15

Drugs should be started at low doses and increased slowly to avoid triggering drug sensitivities common in ME/CFS. ^q16

## Basic facts

ME/CFS affects an estimated 836,000 to 2.5 million Americans but an estimated 84 to 91 percent of patients have not yet been diagnosed. ^q17

ME/CFS affects 3 times as many women as men. Studies have shown one peak of onset between 11-19 and a second between 30-39. ^q18

At least 25% of patients are bedbound or housebound and up to 75% are unable to work or attend school. Symptoms can persist for years, and most patients never regain their pre-disease functioning. ^q19

ME/CFS costs the US $17 to $24 billion annually in lost productivity and direct medical costs. ^q20

The cause of ME/CFS is unknown, although the disease often follows an infection. ^q21

In the past, ME/CFS has been characterized as a syndrome of medically unexplained fatigue responsive to talk therapy and graded exercise. But scientific advances are revealing a complex, multisystem disease involving neurological, immunological, autonomic, and energy metabolism impairments. ^q22
