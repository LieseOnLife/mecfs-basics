>[!warning] Evidence log, not medical advice
>This page explains what research found and what people report. It doesn't recommend treatments. Talk through any change with a clinician who knows ME/CFS.

> [!summary] Quick answer
> Pacing means balancing rest and activity to stay within personal limits, the "energy envelope", so as to avoid post-exertional malaise ([[Post-exertional malaise|PEM]]) and relapses.[[Caring for Patients with ME-CFS Clinical Care and Treatment for Health Care Providers#^q17|CDC 2024]][[Caring for Patients with ME-CFS Clinical Care and Treatment for Health Care Providers#^q18|q18]] Guidelines describe it as person-led and not a cure.[[Myalgic encephalomyelitis (or encephalopathy)-chronic fatigue syndrome diagnosis and management NG206#^q69|NICE 2021]][[Myalgic encephalomyelitis (or encephalopathy)-chronic fatigue syndrome diagnosis and management NG206#^q70|q70]] Going beyond capacity can seriously worsen function,[[Caring for Patients with ME-CFS Clinical Care and Treatment for Health Care Providers#^q20|CDC 2024]] and doing too much on a "good day" can lead to a push-and-crash cycle.[[Caring for Patients with ME-CFS Clinical Care and Treatment for Health Care Providers#^q21|CDC 2024]] For severe illness, care centres on a low-stimulus environment and a detailed care plan,[[Myalgic encephalomyelitis (or encephalopathy)-chronic fatigue syndrome diagnosis and management NG206#^q99|NICE 2021]] [[Guidance on Home-Based Care for People with Severe ME-CFS A Transdisciplinary Expert Statement#^q20|Hermisson et al. 2026]] and caregivers often carry substantial stress and may need support themselves.[[Caring for Patients with ME-CFS Clinical Care and Treatment for Health Care Providers#^q28|CDC 2024]]

## Pacing and the energy envelope
- **What it is:** an individualized way of managing physical, cognitive, and emotional energy within a person's limits by planning where and how to spend it.[^coal-pacing] Every kind of activity uses energy, including conversations, reading, texting, and chores.[^mea-energy]
- **How it's done:** break activities, mental or physical, into small chunks with rest in between;[^afme-chunks] plan rest, including pre-emptive rest;[^nice-plan] and break hard chores such as cleaning, cooking, or showering into shorter, less strenuous pieces.[^cdc-chores]
- **Tracking:** activity logs, heart-rate monitors, and activity trackers can help show when someone is overdoing it,[^coal-pacing] and diaries help identify personal limits, especially early on.[^cdc-diary] NICE suggests making self-monitoring as easy as possible with tools the person already uses.[^nice-monitor]
- **Staying under the limit:** going beyond individual capacity can seriously worsen function and trigger PEM,[^cdc-exceed] and doing too much on a "good day" to make up for lost time can lead to a push-and-crash cycle and relapse.[^cdc-pushcrash]
- **Not fixed increases:** NICE describes energy management as flexible, never increasing activity automatically but holding or adjusting it, upward after a stable period or downward when symptoms worsen.[^nice-flexible] The first step may mean doing less,[^nice-sustainable] and stabilizing can take weeks, months, or years.[^nice-longterm]
- **Not a cure:** NICE calls energy management "not curative",[^nice-em] and Action for ME describes pacing as a way of coping with ME rather than a treatment.[^afme-coping]

In Action for ME's 2019 survey of more than 4,000 people with ME, 88% had tried pacing in the past five years; of those, 70% said they used pacing to do what they felt able to within their manageable limit.[^afme-survey]

They report that it has "definitely helped" with crashes but they haven't recovered. ## During a crash
The Bateman Horne Center's crash guide, written for patients, their support networks, and care teams, describes PEM onset 12 to 72 hours after a trigger, lasting hours to months, and calls prolonged, severe PEM a crash.[^bhc-pem] The guide's advice for caregivers:

- **Rest first:** the guide says the person has to let their body recover before resuming activities, to avoid a repeating push-crash cycle.[^bhc-recover] The caregiver's main goal is to encourage rest, reduce movement, and help them conserve energy.[^bhc-goal]
- **Fewer stimuli:** it advises reducing light, noise, touch, smell, and other distractions.[^bhc-stimuli]
- **Talking may be too much:** the person may not want to or be able to talk; communication cards or devices can be used only as necessary.[^bhc-talk]
- **Letting people know:** it suggests telling the person's primary care doctor and emergency contacts about the crash.[^bhc-contact]
- **When to get help:** severe crashes can be life-threatening if the person can't walk, talk, eat, drink, or sit up,[^bhc-severe] and the guide says a professional medical assessment (911, an emergency room, or urgent care) is best when the person or their caregiver is concerned.[^bhc-911]

## Adapting the home
- **Assessments:** NICE says anyone who needs support at home should get a social care needs assessment covering daily activities, mobility (bed to chair, toilet and washing access, stairs, getting outside), control of light, sound, and temperature, and equipment and adaptations.[^nice-assessment]
- **Aids:** NICE suggests clinicians consider aids such as a wheelchair, blue badge, or stairlift for moderate to very severe ME/CFS.[^nice-aids] The CDC lists recumbent wheelchairs, portable cook surfaces, shower chairs, bedpans, and bedside commodes.[^cdc-aids]
- **The room:** a 2026 expert statement on severe ME/CFS home care puts a quiet location first, then good darkening, ventilation, and climate control.[^herm-room] It advises no perfumes, fragrance-free products depending on tolerance, and avoiding cleaning products where chemical intolerance is severe.[^herm-odors]
- **Hygiene within limits:** the same statement says complete personal hygiene shouldn't be the standard of "good care" if it exceeds the person's energy limits.[^herm-hygiene]

## Caring for someone with severe or very severe ME/CFS
NICE lists what severe and very severe illness can involve: severe constant pain; hypersensitivity to light, sound, touch, movement, temperature, and smells; extreme weakness; trouble speaking or swallowing; and cognitive problems that limit communication.[^nice-severe-symptoms]

- **Pacing becomes the care team's job:** every care task has to be judged by whether it reduces or adds to the person's total burden.[^herm-team] The goal is stabilization, not activation: minimize exertion and delegate tasks where possible.[^herm-stabilize]
- **Exertion adds up:** physical, cognitive, emotional, orthostatic, and sensory demands accumulate; on a day with a doctor's visit, the rest of the day may need to be strict rest.[^herm-accumulate] Rest periods after each care task are part of care.[^herm-rest]
- **Communication:** basic communication can be nonverbal (a call button, gestures, yes/no signals), with a clear "stop" signal after which everything stops and the caregiver leaves the room.[^herm-stop]
- **A detailed care plan** lets every caregiver carry out routines without asking the person to explain again.[^herm-careplan] It should record which familiar doctors to contact in an emergency, how, and whether they do home visits.[^herm-emergency]
- **Body care:** help with regular repositioning may be needed to prevent pressure sores,[^cdc-reposition] and gentle passive range-of-motion work, if touch can be tolerated, can reduce stiffness.[^cdc-rom]
- **Eating:** NICE suggests clinicians consider eating little and often, nourishing drinks and snacks, softer foods, and modified eating aids for severe illness.[^nice-eating]
- **If hospital is needed:** NICE asks hospitals to plan the transfer to minimize PEM, aim for a single room, and keep lights dim, sound low, temperature stable, and smells minimal.[^nice-hospital]

## Looking after yourself as a carer
- **Strain is common:** carers often have substantial stress,[^cdc-carers] and the ME Association and 25% ME Group describe "carer strain" and being too short of time to look after their own health, sometimes alongside financial stress.[^meacarers-strain]
- **Breaks and support:** the ME Association urges carers to keep or build a support network and take breaks.[^mea-network] The expert statement says stabilizing the care team matters as much as medical care, and that self-help groups help.[^herm-limits] It also calls for watching for caregiver burnout and arranging respite and counselling where possible.[^herm-burnout]
- **Peer contact and counselling:** carers in the 25% ME Group's network said contact with other carers helped them feel less alone,[^meacarers-peer] and carers in their focus group found counselling more helpful when the counsellor understood ME/CFS.[^meacarers-counselling]
- **Formal help (UK examples):** registering as a carer with a GP practice,[^meacarers-gp] a carer's assessment,[^meacarers-assessment] home-care cover so the carer can go out,[^meacarers-cover] and respite care.[^meacarers-respite] These are UK services, described by UK charities.
- **Moving and handling:** techniques and aids such as slide sheets help prevent carer injury with bedbound patients; an occupational therapist can assess aids and adaptations.[^meacarers-handling]

> [!quote] 🗣️ Lived experience: r/mecfs, 2026
> A person with very severe ME/CFS who depends on their father writes: "Every day I force myself to suppress emotions I can’t really control because I want to be kinder and easier to care for." They describe his care as sometimes life-sustaining and sometimes harmful, and find it hard to tell carer burnout from neglect. It's a reminder that strain affects both people.[[Very severe ME - bedbound and dependent on father#^q2|r/mecfs, 2026]][[Very severe ME - bedbound and dependent on father#^q8|q8]]
