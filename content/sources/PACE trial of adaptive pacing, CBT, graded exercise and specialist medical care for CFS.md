## Summary
PACE was a UK multicentre randomised trial in secondary-care clinics. It compared specialist medical care (SMC) alone with SMC plus adaptive pacing therapy (APT), SMC plus cognitive behaviour therapy (CBT), or SMC plus graded exercise therapy (GET) in 641 adults selected by the Oxford criteria. The authors reported that CBT and GET added to SMC moderately improved self-rated fatigue and physical function at 52 weeks, that APT did not, and that serious adverse reactions were rare in all arms. It is the largest trial behind earlier CBT/GET recommendations, and later reanalyses and guideline reviews contested its conclusions.

## Methods
- **Design:** parallel-group, four-arm randomised trial at six UK specialist CFS clinics. Recruitment ran March 2005 to November 2008, with follow-up to 52 weeks. Registered as ISRCTN54285094 (not on ClinicalTrials.gov). Allocation was computer-generated and stratified by centre, by whether participants met alternative CFS/ME criteria, and by depressive disorder.
- **Case definition:** Oxford criteria (fatigue as the main symptom with significant disability), which do not require post-exertional malaise (PEM). Entry also required a Chalder fatigue bimodal score of 6/11 or more and an SF-36 physical function score of 60 or less. That threshold was raised to 65 eleven months into the trial to increase recruitment. Participants were also assessed against the "international" criteria (Reeves 2003 revision of CDC 1994, n=427) and the London ME criteria version 2 (n=329) for subgroup analyses. The London criteria require post-exertional fatigue. People unable to attend hospital were excluded.
- **Arms:** 160 APT, 161 CBT, 160 GET, 160 SMC alone. Therapies were manualised, with up to 14 sessions over 23 weeks plus a booster at 36 weeks. The paper describes APT as based on an "envelope" theory, with planning activity to stay within about 70% of perceived energy. CBT was based on a fear-avoidance model and GET on deconditioning and exercise-intolerance models.
- **Outcomes:** the primary outcomes were self-rated Chalder fatigue (Likert, 0–33) and SF-36 physical function (0–100) up to 52 weeks. Participants could not be masked. Before examining outcome data, the investigators changed the Chalder scoring from bimodal to Likert and replaced the originally planned composite improvement measures with continuous scores. Safety outcomes were serious adverse events (SAEs), serious adverse reactions (SARs), serious deterioration, and non-serious adverse events. Secondary outcomes included the 6-minute walk, clinical global impression, work and social adjustment, and PEM symptoms.
- **Funding:** UK Medical Research Council, Department of Health for England, Scottish Chief Scientist Office, and the Department for Work and Pensions.

## Findings
- **Fatigue at 52 weeks vs SMC alone:** 3.4 points lower with CBT (95% CI 1.8–5.0) and 3.2 points lower with GET (1.7–4.8). APT did not differ (0.7 lower, p=0.38).
- **Physical function vs SMC alone:** 7.1 points higher with CBT (2.0–12.1) and 9.4 points higher with GET (4.4–14.4). APT did not differ (3.4 points lower, p=0.18). CBT and GET were each better than APT on both primary outcomes.
- **Within normal ranges on both primary outcomes at 52 weeks (post hoc):** 30% CBT, 28% GET, 16% APT, 15% SMC.
- **Secondary outcomes:** the 6-minute walk favoured GET over CBT, and CBT had the largest reduction in depression scores. The authors report greater reductions in PEM symptoms with CBT and GET than with APT or SMC.
- **Subgroups:** results were described as equivalent in participants who met the international CFS criteria and the London ME criteria.
- **Safety:** serious adverse reactions occurred in 2/159 (APT), 3/161 (CBT), 2/160 (GET), and 2/160 (SMC). SAEs were more frequent with GET than with SMC (p=0.0433); the authors attributed this to events their independent scrutinisers judged unrelated to treatment. Rates of serious deterioration did not differ between arms.
- **Authors' interpretation:** CBT and GET "can safely be added to SMC to moderately improve outcomes", and APT "is not an effective addition".

## Limitations / flags
- **No PEM requirement:** the cohort was selected by the Oxford definition (`no-pem-criterion`). NICE's 2021 evidence review treated PACE evidence as indirect because fewer than 95% of participants were judged to have PEM. See 🔬NICE NG206 evidence review G non-pharmacological management of ME-CFS -by- National Institute for Health and Care Excellence.
- **Outcome measurement:** the primary outcomes were self-reported by unmasked participants. Several analysis changes were made after the protocol but before outcome data were examined: the scoring change, the switch from composite to continuous primary outcomes, and the change in entry threshold.
- **Generalisability:** the trial excluded people who could not attend hospital, so it does not cover housebound or severe ME/CFS.
- **Declared conflicts** (`conflict-of-interest`): the conflicts section lists consultancy for the UK Departments of Health and Work and Pensions and Swiss Re (PDW), work for insurance companies (JB, TC, MS), and book royalties for several authors. The Department for Work and Pensions was a funder.
- **Later reanalysis:** a protocol-based reanalysis of the primary outcomes using FOI-released data reported smaller, mostly non-significant effects. See [[Rethinking the treatment of CFS - a reanalysis of the PACE trial|Rethinking the treatment of chronic fatigue syndrome—a reanalysis and evaluation of findings from a recent major trial of graded exercise and CBT]].
- **Lineage:** this is a PACE-era CBT/GET source (`pace-lineage`). No retraction or correction is listed in Crossref or PubMed as of 2026-11-06.

## Abstract
BACKGROUND: Trial findings show cognitive behaviour therapy (CBT) and graded exercise therapy (GET) can be effective treatments for chronic fatigue syndrome, but patients' organisations have reported that these treatments can be harmful and favour pacing and specialist health care. We aimed to assess effectiveness and safety of all four treatments.

METHODS: In our parallel-group randomised trial, patients meeting Oxford criteria for chronic fatigue syndrome were recruited from six secondary-care clinics in the UK and randomly allocated by computer-generated sequence to receive specialist medical care (SMC) alone or with adaptive pacing therapy (APT), CBT, or GET. Primary outcomes were fatigue (measured by Chalder fatigue questionnaire score) and physical function (measured by short form-36 subscale score) up to 52 weeks after randomisation, and safety was assessed primarily by recording all serious adverse events, including serious adverse reactions to trial treatments. Primary outcomes were rated by participants, who were necessarily unmasked to treatment assignment; the statistician was masked to treatment assignment for the analysis of primary outcomes. We used longitudinal regression models to compare SMC alone with other treatments, APT with CBT, and APT with GET. The final analysis included all participants for whom we had data for primary outcomes. This trial is registered at http://isrctn.org, number ISRCTN54285094. ^q7

FINDINGS: We recruited 641 eligible patients, of whom 160 were assigned to the APT group, 161 to the CBT group, 160 to the GET group, and 160 to the SMC-alone group. Compared with SMC alone, mean fatigue scores at 52 weeks were 3.4 (95% CI 1.8 to 5.0) points lower for CBT (p = 0.0001) and 3.2 (1.7 to 4.8) points lower for GET (p = 0.0003), but did not differ for APT (0.7 [-0.9 to 2.3] points lower; p = 0.38). Compared with SMC alone, mean physical function scores were 7.1 (2.0 to 12.1) points higher for CBT (p = 0.0068) and 9.4 (4.4 to 14.4) points higher for GET (p = 0.0005), but did not differ for APT (3.4 [-1.6 to 8.4] points lower; p=0.18). Compared with APT, CBT and GET were associated with less fatigue (CBT p = 0.0027; GET p = 0.0059) and better physical function (CBT p=0.0002; GET p<0.0001). Subgroup analysis of 427 participants meeting international criteria for chronic fatigue syndrome and 329 participants meeting London criteria for myalgic encephalomyelitis yielded equivalent results. Serious adverse reactions were recorded in two (1%) of 159 participants in the APT group, three (2%) of 161 in the CBT group, two (1%) of 160 in the GET group, and two (1%) of 160 in the SMC-alone group.

INTERPRETATION: CBT and GET can safely be added to SMC to moderately improve outcomes for chronic fatigue syndrome, but APT is not an effective addition.

FUNDING: UK Medical Research Council, Department of Health for England, Scottish Chief Scientist Office, Department for Work and Pensions.

## Full text

### Methods and case definition
PACE was a parallel, four group, multicentre, randomised trial, with outcomes assessed up to 52 weeks after randomisation for patients with chronic fatigue syndrome. We recruited 641 participants from consecutive new outpatients attending six specialist chronic fatigue syndrome clinics in the UK National Health Service between March 18, 2005, and Nov 28, 2008, and completed outcome data collection in January, 2010. ^q1

We selected participants in accordance with Oxford criteria for chronic fatigue syndrome. These criteria require fatigue to be the main symptom, accompanied by significant disability, in the absence of an exclusionary medical or psychiatric diagnosis (psychosis, bipolar disorder, substance misuse, an organic brain disorder, or an eating disorder). Other eligibility criteria consisted of a bimodal score of 6 of 11 or more on the Chalder fatigue questionnaire and a score of 60 of 100 or less on the short form-36 physical function subscale. 11 months after the trial began, this requirement was changed from a score of 60 to a score of 65 to increase recruitment. ^q2

We excluded patients who were younger than 18 years or at significant risk of self-harm, unable to attend hospital appointments, unable to speak and read English, had medical needs that made participation inappropriate, had previously received a trial treatment for their present illness at a PACE trial clinic. Participants were also assessed by international criteria for chronic fatigue syndrome, requiring four or more accompanying symptoms, and the London criteria for myalgic encephalomyelitis (version 2), requiring postexertional fatigue, poor memory and concentration, symptoms that fluctuate, and no primary depressive or anxiety disorder (interpreted as an absence of any such disorder). ^q3

As with any therapy trial, participants, therapists, and doctors could not be masked to treatment allocation and it was also impractical to mask research assessors. The primary outcomes were rated by participants themselves. The statistician undertaking the analysis of primary outcomes was masked to treatment allocation. ^q4

### Findings
We recruited 641 eligible patients, of whom 160 were assigned to the APT group, 161 to the CBT group, 160 to the GET group, and 160 to the SMC-alone group. Compared with SMC alone, mean fatigue scores at 52 weeks were 3·4 (95% CI 1·8 to 5·0) points lower for CBT (p=0·0001) and 3·2 (1·7 to 4·8) points lower for GET (p=0·0003), but did not differ for APT (0·7 [−0·9 to 2·3] points lower; p=0·38). Compared with SMC alone, mean physical function scores were 7·1 (2·0 to 12·1) points higher for CBT (p=0·0068) and 9·4 (4·4 to 14·4) points higher for GET (p=0·0005), but did not differ for APT (3·4 [−1·6 to 8·4] points lower; p=0·18). Compared with APT, CBT and GET were associated with less fatigue (CBT p=0·0027; GET p=0·0059) and better physical function (CBT p=0·0002; GET p<0·0001). Subgroup analysis of 427 participants meeting international criteria for chronic fatigue syndrome and 329 participants meeting London criteria for myalgic encephalomyelitis yielded equivalent results. Serious adverse reactions were recorded in two (1%) of 159 participants in the APT group, three (2%) of 161 in the CBT group, two (1%) of 160 in the GET group, and two (1%) of 160 in the SMC-alone group. ^q5

### Interpretation
CBT and GET can safely be added to SMC to moderately improve outcomes for chronic fatigue syndrome, but APT is not an effective addition. ^q6
