## Summary
This CDC National Center for Health Statistics report estimates how many US adults had ME/CFS in 2021–2022, using the nationally representative National Health Interview Survey (NHIS). It reports that 1.3% of adults said a clinician had diagnosed them with ME/CFS and that they still had it. Estimates were higher in women, in people aged 50–69, in White adults, in lower-income families, and in rural areas.

## Methods
- **Source:** NCHS Data Brief No. 488 (December 2023), by Anjel Vahratian (NCHS Division of Health Interview Statistics) with Jin-Mann S. Lin, Jeanne Bertolli, and Elizabeth R. Unger (CDC Division of High Consequence Pathogens and Pathology). The page was first published 2023-12-08 and lists "Last reviewed: December 7, 2023". It is indexed in PubMed (PMID 38085820). It is a federal statistical report, not a clinical guideline.
- **Design:** cross-sectional household survey of the US civilian noninstitutionalized population. NHIS data came from 57,133 adults (29,482 in 2021 and 27,651 in 2022). Because of the pandemic, 62.8% (2021) and 55.7% (2022) of interviews were done at least partly by telephone.
- **Case ascertainment:** a "yes" to both "Have you ever been told by a doctor or other health professional that you had Chronic Fatigue Syndrome (CFS) or Myalgic Encephalomyelitis (ME)?" and "Do you still have Chronic Fatigue Syndrome (CFS) or ME?" No research case definition was applied and PEM was not asked about.
- **Analysis:** SUDAAN weighting for the complex sample design, Korn–Graubard confidence intervals, and two-sided tests of differences and linear or quadratic trends at the 0.05 level. Breakdowns were by sex, age, race and Hispanic origin, family income relative to the federal poverty level, and NCHS urban–rural classification.

## Findings
- **Overall:** 1.3% of adults (95% CI 1.2–1.4) had ME/CFS.
- **Sex:** women 1.7% (1.5–1.8) vs men 0.9% (0.8–1.0).
- **Age:** 0.7% at ages 18–39, 1.2% at 40–49, 2.0% at 50–59, and 2.1% at 60–69, then 1.4% at 70 and older (a significant quadratic trend).
- **Race and Hispanic origin:** White non-Hispanic 1.5%, Black non-Hispanic 1.2%, Hispanic 0.8%, and Asian non-Hispanic 0.7%. White adults differed significantly from Hispanic and Asian adults, and Black adults from Asian adults.
- **Income:** 2.0% below the federal poverty level, 1.7% at 100–199% of it, and 1.1% at 200% or more (a linear trend).
- **Urbanization:** 1.0% in large central metro areas, 1.1% in large fringe metro areas, 1.5% in medium and small metro areas, and 1.9% in nonmetropolitan areas (a linear trend).
- The authors note that the estimates count only diagnosed cases, so true prevalence may be higher.

## Limitations
- Self-reported diagnosis only, with no clinical confirmation and no named case definition or PEM requirement. Misdiagnosis (people told they have CFS without meeting PEM-based criteria) and missed diagnoses (undiagnosed cases, which the authors say are common) are both possible.
- Household survey of the noninstitutionalized population: people in care facilities are excluded, and the most severely ill may be under-represented.
- Cross-sectional and descriptive. The brief does not separate post-COVID from pre-pandemic onset, report illness duration or severity, or adjust group comparisons for other factors.
- Estimates for other racial groups were too unreliable to publish separately; they are included in the totals only.
- No treatment is studied.

## Abstract
Myalgic encephalomyelitis/chronic fatigue syndrome (ME/CFS) is a complex, multisystem illness characterized by activity-limiting fatigue, worsening of symptoms after activity, and other symptoms. It affects all age, sex, and racial and ethnic groups and costs the U.S. economy about $18–$51 billion annually. This report describes the percentage of adults who had ME/CFS at the time of interview by selected demographic and geographic characteristics based on data from the 2021–2022 National Health Interview Survey (NHIS). ^q1

Key findings (from the report): In 2021–2022, 1.3% of adults had ME/CFS. The percentage of adults who had ME/CFS increased with age through ages 60–69 and then declined among those age 70 and older. White non-Hispanic (1.5%) adults were more likely to have ME/CFS compared with Asian non-Hispanic (0.7%) and Hispanic (0.8%) adults. Adults with a family income less than 100% of the federal poverty level (2.0%) were more likely to have ME/CFS, followed by those at 100–199% (1.7%), and those at or above 200% (1.1%). The percentage of adults who had ME/CFS increased with increasing rurality of their place of residence. ^q2

### Data source and methods
NHIS data from 57,133 adults (29,482 in 2021 and 27,651 in 2022) were used for this analysis. NHIS is a nationally representative household survey of the civilian noninstitutionalized U.S. population. It is conducted continuously throughout the year by the National Center for Health Statistics. Interviews are typically conducted in respondents’ homes, but follow-ups to complete interviews may be conducted over the telephone. Due to data collection disruptions caused by the COVID-19 pandemic in 2021 and 2022, 62.8% and 55.7% of the adult interviews were conducted at least partially by telephone, respectively. Point estimates and the corresponding confidence intervals for this analysis were calculated using SAS-callable SUDAAN software to account for the complex sample design of NHIS. All estimates are based on self-report. ^q6

### Case ascertainment and limitation
ME/CFS status was based on a yes response to both of the following survey questions: “Have you ever been told by a doctor or other health professional that you had Chronic Fatigue Syndrome (CFS) or Myalgic Encephalomyelitis (ME)?” and “Do you still have Chronic Fatigue Syndrome (CFS) or ME?” ^q7

As the prevalence estimates in this report are based on a doctor’s diagnosis, adults who are undiagnosed are not included in this analysis. True prevalence estimates may be higher, as previous studies suggest that many people with ME/CFS are undiagnosed. ^q8
