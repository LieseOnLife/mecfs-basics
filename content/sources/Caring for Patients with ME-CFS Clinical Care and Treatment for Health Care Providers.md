## Summary
CDC's "Clinical Care and Treatment" section for health care providers is a hub page, "Caring for Patients with ME/CFS", with four subpages:
- Strategies to Prevent Worsening of Symptoms (the symptom-management page);
- Monitoring the Use of Medicines and Supplements;
- ME/CFS Clinical Care for Severely Affected Patients;
- Considerations for Pediatric ME/CFS.

The pages advise managing the symptoms the patient finds most disruptive first and preventing post-exertional malaise through activity management (pacing). They warn that standard exercise advice for healthy people can substantially harm patients with ME/CFS. CDC's provider diagnosis page uses the IOM 2015 criteria.

## Findings
- **PEM.** CDC describes symptoms worsening 12 to 48 hours after activity and lasting days or weeks. It frames activity management (pacing) as balancing rest and activity to stay within individual limits (the "energy envelope"), with diaries to find those limits. It warns against "push and crash" cycles.
- **Activity and exercise.** Activity or exercise plans should be individualized and designed with the patient's input, and evaluation by a rehabilitation specialist may help. Vigorous aerobic exercise is not tolerated, and standard exercise recommendations for healthy people "can substantially harm" patients. Orthostatic intolerance may need treatment first. Patients who tolerate their current level might carefully increase exercise, and some clinicians refer to an exercise physiologist who understands ME/CFS. Exercise is described as not a cure.
- **Orthostatic intolerance.** CDC suggests asking screening questions and doing standing tests (the NASA lean test or tilt table). Strategies include avoiding triggers, postural counter-maneuvers, compression or cooling garments, and more salt and fluid where intake is low and not contraindicated. Prescription medicines for POTS or neurally mediated hypotension come next if those measures fail; no specific drugs are named.
- **Sleep.** After sleep hygiene, over-the-counter products come first, then prescription sleep medicine at the lowest dose for the shortest time. A sleep study or sleep-specialist referral is suggested when indicated.
- **Pain.** Over-the-counter analgesics come first, then pain-specialist referral. Non-drug options include stretching and movement therapies, manual physical therapy, gentle massage, heat, toning exercises, hydrotherapy, and acupuncture, with caution to avoid PEM.
- **Memory and concentration.** Memory aids and planned rest after mental activity are suggested. Caution is advised with stimulants: mild stimulants may help some patients, but stronger ones can lead to push-crash relapse, and many are habit-forming.
- **Depression and anxiety.** These are treated as comorbid conditions. Antidepressants should start low because some can worsen ME/CFS symptoms, such as sedation or orthostatic hypotension. The page says treating these conditions does not cure ME/CFS.
- **Medicines and supplements.** Patients are often sensitive to medicines, especially those acting on the central nervous system, and may need lower-than-standard doses. Tricyclics may help mood, sleep, and pain but can worsen orthostatic intolerance. Supplements are unregulated and should be discussed, and many treatments promoted as cures are unproven, costly, and could be dangerous.
- **Severely affected patients.** CDC estimates up to 1 in 4 patients is home- or bed-bound at some time. Care includes home visits and remote check-ups, a quiet darkened environment, minimal medication, assistive equipment, passive range-of-motion exercise, help with repositioning to prevent pressure sores, and counseling. Complete bed rest carries risks and should be reserved for the most severe cases.
- **Children.** CDC suggests extra caution with medicines (smallest dose, shortest time) and school accommodations communicated through clinician letters, with attention to HIPAA and FERPA.

## Limitations
- The CDC site states that its ME/CFS content is "for informational purposes only and does not represent a federal guideline or recommendation for the treatment of ME/CFS." It is not a formal graded guideline and cites no evidence review on the pages reviewed.
- The pages name few specific drugs. The treatments they name or describe directly are pacing, compression garments, salt and fluid, stimulants, tricyclics, acupuncture, and physical therapy.
- These are living web pages and may change after the 2024 review dates above.

## Abstract
Key points from the hub page: ME/CFS is a chronic, life-altering disease affecting many organ systems that profoundly affects quality of life and abilities. Clinicians can learn about pediatric considerations, strategies for preventing worsening of symptoms, monitoring medicines and supplements, and clinical care for severely affected patients.

## Quoted passages

### Caring for Patients with ME/CFS

- Myalgic encephalomyelitis/chronic fatigue syndrome (ME/CFS) is a chronic, life-altering disease affecting many organ systems. ^q1

### Diagnosing ME/CFS

There is no confirmatory test currently available to diagnose ME/CFS. However, people with ME/CFS often are found to have biological abnormalities. Yet none of these “biomarkers” have sufficient sensitivity and specificity to constitute a diagnostic test. Instead, healthcare providers need to diagnose ME/CFS based on a thorough medical history and physical exam, laboratory test results, and ruling out other fatiguing illnesses with a targeted work-up. ^q4

- impaired function accompanied by fatigue ^q5
- post-exertional malaise (PEM) ^q6
- unrefreshing sleep ^q7
- either one or both
  * orthostatic intolerance. ^q8

A thorough medical history, physical (including neurological) and mental health exam, and laboratory testing should be performed. These steps are crucial to evaluate for other conditions that may explain symptoms. During the evaluation period, healthcare providers may also begin to consider ME/CFS and schedule periodic follow-up appointments. All symptoms should be addressed and managed and any identified comorbidities treated to improve quality of life. ^q9

If symptoms persist for six months or longer, ME/CFS is diagnosed. Patients with ME/CFS should be re-evaluated periodically. Routine age-appropriate health monitoring/screening is important and should continue. ^q10

Getting detailed medical history and social and occupational histories is critical to assess effects of ME/CFS on a patient's daily functioning. PEM may be delayed after onset of other symptoms, and some patients may not recognize PEM at the start of illness. ^q11

Consider asking patients to document their activities and symptoms for a couple of weeks in a diary. ^q12

Patients with unexplained impaired function with fatigue for 6 months or longer who do not fully meet the ME/CFS case definition can be managed like patients with ME/CFS and monitored for change. ^q13

### IOM 2015 diagnostic criteria

The criteria state that three required symptoms and at least one of two additional symptoms are required for diagnosis. ^q14

### Post-exertional malaise and activity management

Post-exertional malaise (PEM) is the worsening of symptoms following even minor physical or mental exertion that would have been tolerated previously. Symptoms typically worsen 12 to 48 hours after activity. PEM can last for days or even weeks. ^q16

PEM can be mitigated by activity management (pacing). The goal is to avoid PEM flare-ups and illness relapses by balancing rest and activity. ^q17

Patients need to determine their individual limits for mental and physical activity. Then, they can plan activity and rest to stay within these limits. Some patients and healthcare providers refer to staying within these limits as staying within the "energy envelope." ^q18

Limitations may be different for each patient. Keeping individual activity and symptom diaries may help patients identify their personal limitations, especially early in clinical care. ^q19

Healthcare providers need to be aware that their patients' ability to function may seriously deteriorate if they exceed their individual capacities. In general, patients should not push themselves beyond their capacities as this may exacerbate their symptoms and trigger PEM. ^q20

When patients improve, activities can be cautiously increased with monitoring for any negative effects. Patients need to be advised about "push and crash" cycles. This occurs when patients try to do too much on a "good day" to make up for "lost time." This pushing can lead to relapse. ^q21

For some patients, even daily chores and activities such as cleaning, cooking, or showering can be difficult. These tasks may need to be broken down into shorter, less strenuous pieces. ^q23

While vigorous aerobic exercise can be beneficial for many chronic illnesses, patients with ME/CFS cannot tolerate such exercise routines. Standard exercise recommendations for healthy people can substantially harm patients with ME/CFS. ^q24

### Care for severely affected patients

It's estimated up to 1 in 4 ME/CFS patients is home- or bed-bound at some time in their illness. ^q26

These patients often cannot make office visits due to the severity of their symptoms. They require in-home assistance and management plans specifically adjusted to their needs by a coordinated care team of providers. Caregivers, who provide management and the majority of care for patients, often have substantial stress and may need additional support. ^q28

Some accommodations for those severely ill from ME/CFS include recumbent wheelchairs, portable cook surfaces, shower chairs and bedpans. These tools allow them to consider sitting when cooking or showering. They can use a bed-side commode instead of walking to the restroom. And they can take frequent breaks during activity to save their energy. ^q30

A realistic goal with severely ill patients is focusing on reducing stiffness and maintaining joint range of motion. This can be accomplished by passive range-of-motion exercises and gentle stretching (if touch can be tolerated). ^q31

Help with regular repositioning may be needed to prevent pressure sores in patients are unable to turn over in bed. ^q34

### Medicines and supplements

People with ME/CFS are often more sensitive to medicines than people without ME/CFS. Therapeutic benefits can often be achieved at lower-than-standard doses. People with ME/CFS might tolerate or need only a fraction of the usual recommended medication dose. After initial management with lower dosing, clinicians may consider gradual increases as necessary and as tolerated by the patient. ^q35

People with ME/CFS frequently use vitamins and other nutritional and herbal supplements. These products are unregulated and information on potency and side effects is often unknown. Because of this, healthcare providers and people with ME/CFS need to talk about nutritional and herbal supplements. This should occur before a patient begins taking the supplements if possible. During conversations, consider safety, effectiveness, and possible interactions between prescribed medications and over-the-counter supplements. ^q37

It is important to educate patients about all potential therapies. Be aware that many treatments that are promoted as cures for ME/CFS are unproven, often costly, and could be dangerous. ^q46
