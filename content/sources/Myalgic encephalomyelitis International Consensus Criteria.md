## Summary
This is the original International Consensus Criteria (ICC) paper, published in the Journal of Internal Medicine. It defines myalgic encephalomyelitis (ME) for both clinical and research use, in adults and children. The panel started from the 2003 Canadian Consensus Criteria and made major changes:
- it dropped the 6-month waiting period before diagnosis;
- it made "postexertional neuroimmune exhaustion" (PENE), its term for PEM, compulsory;
- it grouped the remaining symptoms by regions of pathophysiology;
- it recommended using the name ME rather than CFS.

**This is a case definition, not a cohort.** The paper creates the International Consensus Criteria (ICC), which make post-exertional neuroimmune exhaustion (PENE) compulsory.

## Methods
- **Panel.** An International Consensus Panel of clinicians, researchers, teaching faculty, and an independent patient advocate came from 13 countries. The members report about 400 years of combined clinical and teaching experience and roughly 50,000 patients diagnosed or treated.
- **Process.** The panel drew on its own expertise plus PubMed and other medical sources, working through rounds of suggestions, drafts, reviews, and revisions. It reached 100% consensus through a Delphi-type process.
- **Funding.** The paper states it is free of sponsorship; authors contributed on a volunteer basis without payment.
- **Scope.** The paper covers the criteria and their application only.
- **Access.** Open access in PMC (PMC3427890). Published online 22 August 2011 in J Intern Med 2011;270(4):327–338. There is no patient sample.

## Findings
- **Criteria structure (Table 1).** A patient must meet all four groups:
  - **A. PENE (compulsory):**
    - marked, rapid physical and/or cognitive fatigability after exertion that may be minimal;
    - post-exertional symptom exacerbation;
    - exhaustion that may come immediately or be delayed by hours or days;
    - prolonged recovery, usually 24 hours or longer;
    - a low threshold of fatigability that substantially reduces pre-illness activity.
  - **B. Neurological impairments:** at least one symptom from three of four categories:
    - neurocognitive impairments;
    - pain;
    - sleep disturbance;
    - neurosensory, perceptual, and motor disturbances.
  - **C. Immune, gastrointestinal, and genitourinary impairments:** at least one symptom from three of the five categories in this group.
  - **D. Energy production or transportation impairments:** at least one symptom, covering cardiovascular, respiratory, thermostatic, and temperature-intolerance features.
- **Severity.** At least a 50% reduction in pre-illness activity is required. Severity is graded as mild (about 50% reduction), moderate (mostly housebound), severe (mostly bedridden), or very severe (totally bedridden and needing help with basic functions).
- **Atypical ME.** A patient who meets PENE but falls up to two short of the other required symptoms is classed as atypical ME. Pain or sleep disturbance may rarely be absent.
- **Exclusions and comorbidities.** Primary psychiatric disorders, somatoform disorder, and substance abuse are excluded. Listed comorbid entities include fibromyalgia, IBS, interstitial cystitis, migraine, allergies, multiple chemical sensitivities, and reactive depression.
- **Children.** The paper notes that the most prominent symptoms in children tend to be neurological, and that symptom severity fluctuates more than in adults.
- **Clinical application.** The paper suggests assessing symptom clusters and total illness burden, ranking symptom severity periodically, separating primary symptoms from secondary effects and aggravators, and subgrouping by the most severe cluster (neurological, immune, energy metabolism/transport, or eclectic). It includes pediatric notes on interviewing both parents, comparing current activity with pre-illness activity, and not mistaking ME for school phobia.
- **Research application.** Patients should meet the full criteria for epidemiological studies, with any subgroups or atypical ME clearly identified. The authors recommend the International Symptom Scale for consistent data collection, completed before entering a study.

## Limitations
- A 2017 erratum was published (J Intern Med 2017;282(4):353, doi 10.1111/joim.12658, PMID 28929634), and the PMC record carries a "This article has been corrected" notice. The erratum text was not accessible (Europe PMC returned an error and the PDF was blocked), so what it changed is not recorded here.
- This is a consensus definition, not an empirical validation study. The IOM 2015 report notes that the ICC does not give guidance on the severity or frequency of symptoms needed for diagnosis. It also notes that other researchers challenged the choice of the name ME, arguing there is no convincing evidence of brain inflammation.
- No treatments are tested or compared.

## Abstract
The label 'chronic fatigue syndrome' (CFS) has persisted for many years because of the lack of knowledge of the aetiological agents and the disease process. In view of more recent research and clinical experience that strongly point to widespread inflammation and multisystemic neuropathology, it is more appropriate and correct to use the term 'myalgic encephalomyelitis' (ME) because it indicates an underlying pathophysiology. It is also consistent with the neurological classification of ME in the World Health Organization's International Classification of Diseases (ICD G93.3). Consequently, an International Consensus Panel consisting of clinicians, researchers, teaching faculty and an independent patient advocate was formed with the purpose of developing criteria based on current knowledge. Thirteen countries and a wide range of specialties were represented. Collectively, members have approximately 400 years of both clinical and teaching experience, authored hundreds of peer-reviewed publications, diagnosed or treated approximately 50 000 patients with ME, and several members coauthored previous criteria. The expertise and experience of the panel members as well as PubMed and other medical sources were utilized in a progression of suggestions/drafts/reviews/revisions. The authors, free of any sponsoring organization, achieved 100% consensus through a Delphi-type process. The scope of this paper is limited to criteria of ME and their application. Accordingly, the criteria reflect the complex symptomatology. Operational notes enhance clarity and specificity by providing guidance in the expression and interpretation of symptoms. Clinical and research application guidelines promote optimal recognition of ME by primary physicians and other healthcare providers, improve the consistency of diagnoses in adult and paediatric patients internationally and facilitate clearer identification of patients for research studies. ^q1

## Quoted passages

### Core requirements
A patient will meet the criteria for postexertional neuroimmune exhaustion (A), at least one symptom from three neurological impairment categories (B), at least one symptom from three immune/gastro-intestinal/genitourinary impairment categories (C), and at least one symptom from energy metabolism/transport impairments (D). ^q2

### A. Postexertional neuroimmune exhaustion (PENE): compulsory
This cardinal feature is a pathological inability to produce sufficient energy on demand with prominent symptoms primarily in the neuroimmune regions. Characteristics are as follows: marked, rapid physical and/or cognitive fatigability in response to exertion, which may be minimal such as activities of daily living or simple mental tasks; postexertional symptom exacerbation, e.g. acute flu-like symptoms, pain and worsening of other symptoms; postexertional exhaustion may occur immediately after activity or be delayed by hours or days; recovery period is prolonged, usually taking 24 h or longer, and a relapse can last days, weeks or longer; low threshold of physical and mental fatigability (lack of stamina) results in a substantial reduction in pre-illness activity level. ^q3
