## Summary
This Cochrane review (CD003200) pooled eight randomised trials of exercise therapy for adults with CFS. It compared exercise with passive controls, CBT, adaptive pacing, and an antidepressant. The authors conclude that exercise therapy "probably has a positive effect on fatigue" compared with usual care or passive therapies at the end of treatment. Long-term effects and harms are uncertain, and all included trials used the CDC 1994 (Fukuda) or Oxford criteria.

**Version status as of 2026-11-06:**
- The current version is pub9 (19 December 2024). Cochrane gave it a new citation to carry an editorial note, and records that "conclusions have not changed". The underlying analysis is the 2019 amended version (pub8, 2 October 2019, PMID 31577366, DOI 10.1002/14651858.CD003200.pub8).
- The editorial note (19 December 2024; amended 31 January and 25 March 2025) says the review includes studies from searches up to 9 May 2014. It also says a pilot project, begun on 2 October 2019, to involve interest holders in developing an update "has now been discontinued".
- A 2 October 2019 editorial note points to the Editor-in-Chief's statement at cochrane.org/news/cfs.

## Methods
- **Search:** Cochrane Common Mental Disorders register, CENTRAL, SPORTDiscus, and the WHO trials registry up to May 2014, plus reference lists and expert contact.
- **Eligibility:** RCTs in adults with a primary diagnosis of CFS "from all diagnostic criteria" who could take part in exercise therapy.
- **Included trials:** eight, with 1,518 participants: Fulcher 1997, Jason 2007, Moss-Morris 2005, Powell 2001, Wallman 2004, Wearden 1998, Wearden 2010 (FINE), and White 2011 (PACE).
  - Seven used aerobic exercise (walking, swimming, cycling, or dancing, at intensities from very low to quite rigorous); one used anaerobic exercise.
  - Programmes lasted 12–26 weeks, with long-term follow-up at 50–72 weeks.
- **Case definitions:** all trials used CDC 1994 (Fukuda), Oxford, or both. Neither definition requires PEM.
- **Comparators:** passive control (treatment as usual, relaxation, or flexibility), CBT (2 trials), cognitive therapy, supportive listening, adaptive pacing (PACE only), and fluoxetine (Wearden 1998, alone or combined).
- **Analysis:** SMDs were re-expressed on the 0–33 Chalder scale, risk ratios were used for dichotomous outcomes, and certainty was rated with GRADE. All trials had high risk of performance and detection bias; most had low risk of selection bias.
- **Funding:** internal support from the University of Oxford Department of Psychiatry and the Norwegian Knowledge Centre. The authors declare nothing to disclose.

## Findings
**Exercise vs passive control:**
- Fatigue at end of treatment: SMD −0.66 (95% CI −1.01 to −0.31; 7 trials, 840 participants; moderate certainty), re-expressed as about 3.4 points lower on the 0–33 scale.
- Long-term fatigue is uncertain: SMD −0.62 (−1.32 to 0.07; 4 trials, 670 participants; very low certainty).
- Serious adverse reactions are uncertain: RR 0.99 (0.14 to 6.97; 1 trial, 319 participants; very low certainty).
- Physical function may moderately improve at end of treatment; the long-term effect is uncertain. Sleep may improve slightly.
- Effects on pain, quality of life, and depression are uncertain or not reported.

**Exercise vs CBT:**
- Little or no difference in fatigue at end of treatment (MD 0.20; 1 trial, 298 participants; low certainty) or long term (SMD 0.07; 2 trials, 351 participants; moderate certainty).
- Serious adverse reactions are uncertain (RR 0.67, 0.11 to 3.96; very low certainty).

**Exercise vs adaptive pacing (PACE only):**
- Fatigue may be slightly lower with exercise: MD −2.00 at end of treatment and −2.50 long term on the 0–33 scale (about 305–307 participants; low certainty).
- Physical function, depression, and sleep may be slightly better with exercise (low certainty).
- Serious adverse reactions are uncertain (very low certainty).

**Exercise vs antidepressant:** effects on fatigue and depression are uncertain (very low certainty), and adverse reactions were not reported.

**Authors' caveat:** patients diagnosed by other criteria "may experience different effects". The authors also say comparative effectiveness against CBT and pacing is hard to judge because of limited evidence.

## Limitations
- **Cohorts:** no included trial required PEM. PACE contributes the largest share of participants and is the only pacing comparison.
- **Outcomes:** all trials were unblinded with mostly self-rated outcomes. The harms evidence rests largely on one trial.
- **2019 amendment:** pub8 was amended after a formal complaint by Robert Courtney and an editorial review. The changes were:
  - fatigue analyses restructured around SMDs;
  - long-term fatigue added to the Summary of findings;
  - the GRADE rating for adverse reactions changed;
  - the criteria used to define CFS acknowledged.
- **Out of date:** the evidence base is dated May 2014, and the planned full update with an independent advisory group was discontinued (see Summary).
- **Critiques:** a published reanalysis of the 2017 version argues that the trials' objective outcomes show no benefit and that harms were under-reported. See [[Graded exercise therapy for ME-CFS is not effective and unsafe - re-analysis of a Cochrane review|Graded exercise therapy for myalgic encephalomyelitis/chronic fatigue syndrome is not effective and unsafe. Re-analysis of a Cochrane review]].
- **NICE:** NICE's 2021 evidence review did not use this review for decision-making because it pooled different exercise types and "passive" control arms. See 🔬NICE NG206 evidence review G non-pharmacological management of ME-CFS -by- National Institute for Health and Care Excellence.
- **Retractions:** Crossref lists no retraction; pub9 supersedes pub8 as a new version.

## Abstract
EDITORIAL NOTE: Editorial note (19 December 2024; amended 31 January 2025; amended 25 March 2025): This Cochrane review was published in 2019 and includes studies from searches up to 9 May 2014. A pilot project for engaging interest holders in the development of an update of this Cochrane review was initiated on 2 October 2019 and has now been discontinued. Editorial note (2 October 2019): A statement from the Editor in Chief about this review and its planned update is available at https://www.cochrane.org/news/cfs

BACKGROUND: Chronic fatigue syndrome (CFS) or myalgic encephalomyelitis (ME) is a serious disorder characterised by persistent postexertional fatigue and substantial symptoms related to cognitive, immune and autonomous dysfunction. There is no specific diagnostic test, therefore diagnostic criteria are used to diagnose CFS. The prevalence of CFS varies by type of diagnostic criteria used. Existing treatment strategies primarily aim to relieve symptoms and improve function. One treatment option is exercise therapy.

OBJECTIVES: The objective of this review was to determine the effects of exercise therapy for adults with CFS compared with any other intervention or control on fatigue, adverse outcomes, pain, physical functioning, quality of life, mood disorders, sleep, self-perceived changes in overall health, health service resources use and dropout.

SEARCH METHODS: We searched the Cochrane Common Mental Disorders Group controlled trials register, CENTRAL, and SPORTDiscus up to May 2014, using a comprehensive list of free-text terms for CFS and exercise. We located unpublished and ongoing studies through the World Health Organization International Clinical Trials Registry Platform up to May 2014. We screened reference lists of retrieved articles and contacted experts in the field for additional studies. ^q1

SELECTION CRITERIA: We included randomised controlled trials (RCTs) about adults with a primary diagnosis of CFS, from all diagnostic criteria, who were able to participate in exercise therapy. ^q2

DATA COLLECTION AND ANALYSIS: Two review authors independently performed study selection, 'Risk of bias' assessments and data extraction. We combined continuous measures of outcomes using mean differences (MDs) or standardised mean differences (SMDs). To facilitate interpretation of SMDs, we re-expressed SMD estimates as MDs on more common measurement scales. We combined dichotomous outcomes using risk ratios (RRs). We assessed the certainty of evidence using GRADE.

MAIN RESULTS: We included eight RCTs with data from 1518 participants. Exercise therapy lasted from 12 weeks to 26 weeks. The studies measured effect at the end of the treatment and at long-term follow-up, after 50 weeks or 72 weeks. Seven studies used aerobic exercise therapies such as walking, swimming, cycling or dancing, provided at mixed levels in terms of intensity of the aerobic exercise from very low to quite rigorous, and one study used anaerobic exercise. Control groups consisted of passive control, including treatment as usual, relaxation or flexibility (eight studies); cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) (two studies); cognitive therapy (one study); supportive listening (one study); pacing (one study); pharmacological treatment (one study) and combination treatment (one study). Most studies had a low risk of selection bias. All had a high risk of performance and detection bias. Exercise therapy compared with 'passive' control: Exercise therapy probably reduces fatigue at end of treatment (SMD -0.66, 95% CI -1.01 to -0.31; 7 studies, 840 participants; moderate-certainty evidence; re-expressed MD -3.4, 95% CI -5.3 to -1.6; scale 0 to 33). We are uncertain if fatigue is reduced in the long term because the certainty of the evidence is very low (SMD -0.62, 95% CI -1.32 to 0.07; 4 studies, 670 participants; re-expressed MD -3.2, 95% CI -6.9 to 0.4; scale 0 to 33). We are uncertain about the risk of serious adverse reactions because the certainty of the evidence is very low (RR 0.99, 95% CI 0.14 to 6.97; 1 study, 319 participants). Exercise therapy may moderately improve physical functioning at end of treatment, but the long-term effect is uncertain because the certainty of the evidence is very low. Exercise therapy may also slightly improve sleep at end of treatment and at long term. The effect of exercise therapy on pain, quality of life and depression is uncertain because evidence is missing or of very low certainty. Exercise therapy compared with CBT: Exercise therapy may make little or no difference to fatigue at end of treatment (MD 0.20, 95% CI -1.49 to 1.89; 1 study, 298 participants; low-certainty evidence), or at long-term follow-up (SMD 0.07, 95% CI -0.13 to 0.28; 2 studies, 351 participants; moderate-certainty evidence). We are uncertain about the risk of serious adverse reactions because the certainty of the evidence is very low (RR 0.67, 95% CI 0.11 to 3.96; 1 study, 321 participants). ^q3

AUTHORS' CONCLUSIONS: Exercise therapy probably has a positive effect on fatigue in adults with CFS compared to usual care or passive therapies. The evidence regarding adverse effects is uncertain. Due to limited evidence it is difficult to draw conclusions about the comparative effectiveness of CBT, adaptive pacing or other interventions. All studies were conducted with outpatients diagnosed with 1994 criteria of the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention or the Oxford criteria, or both. Patients diagnosed using other criteria may experience different effects. ^q4
