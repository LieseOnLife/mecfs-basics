## Summary
This is an independent analysis of the UK ME Association's 2015 online patient survey. It asked people with ME/CFS whether their symptoms got better, stayed the same, or got worse after a course of CBT, graded exercise therapy (GET), or pacing therapy. The authors compare the results with ten earlier patient surveys (2000–2015). Across the primary and secondary surveys, respondents most often reported worsening after GET and improvement after pacing. CBT was mostly reported as making no difference. The design is a self-selected, retrospective, cross-sectional survey, so these are reported experiences, not measured treatment effects.

## Methods
- **Data:** an online survey open for 4 months and advertised nationally, not only to ME Association members. It had 228 questions and 1,428 respondents. Respondents had to confirm a diagnosis of ME/CFS from a qualified medical professional and that they had attended a one-to-one or group CBT, GET, or pacing course.
- **Analysis cohort:** 954 respondents who confirmed a diagnosis, had attended at least one course, and rated symptoms and illness severity before and after. Another 21 were dropped from the course-composition table for ambiguous answers, leaving 933.
- **Case definition:** none applied. Diagnosis was self-reported, with 72% diagnosed by a specialist, 22% by a GP, and 4.5% by another professional. No criteria set (Fukuda, CCC, ICC, IOM) was used, so the cohort did not require PEM.
- **Respondents:** 79% female. Mean onset age was about 35 and illness duration about 6 years; 49% had been ill more than 10 years.
- **Analysis:** descriptive tables and ordered logistic regression of symptom change (worse / same / improved) on course composition. Mixed courses were weighted (80% to the main course, with sensitivity checks). The models adjusted for demographic, illness, and course variables.
- **Secondary surveys:** a literature and grey-literature search found more than 15 patient surveys. Ten were included (UK, Norway, Netherlands, Scotland; 2001–2015), and crude improved/unchanged/worse rates were aggregated.
- **Funding and disclosures:** the ME Association gave access to the raw dataset and agreed a small post-publication award to cover costs. The authors declare no conflicts of interest.

## Findings
**Primary survey, main course only** (symptom change after the course):

| Course | n | Worse | Same | Improved |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| CBT only | 179 | 18% | 73% | 8% |
| GET only | 77 | 74% | 14% | 12% |
| Pacing only | 70 | 14% | 41% | 44% |

- **Any combination:** CBT-led courses were 23% worse, 65% same, 12% improved (n=490). GET-led were 65% worse, 20% same, 15% improved (n=224). Pacing-led were 20% worse, 43% same, 37% improved (n=219).
- **Overall (n=933):** 32% worse, 49% same, 19% improved.
- **Regression:** a greater share of GET in a course was associated with worse reported symptom change (β −0.32 unadjusted, −0.22 adjusted). A greater share of pacing was associated with improvement (β +0.22 unadjusted, +0.12 adjusted). CBT showed no significant association.
- **Other associations:** the strongest independent association was whether respondents felt the course was appropriate. Those who rated it "not appropriate" mostly reported worsening. Not completing the course, longer illness duration, and female sex were also associated with worse reported change.
- **Therapist beliefs:** when respondents said their therapist regarded ME/CFS as psychological, 55% reported worsening and 2% improvement. When the therapist regarded it as physical, 19% reported worsening and 33% improvement.
- **Appropriateness:** 78% rated GET not appropriate to their needs, and 84% rated pacing appropriate or partly appropriate.

**Secondary surveys, weighted aggregates** (ten surveys, n=16,665 response sets):

| Treatment | Improved | No change | Worse |
|---|---|---|---|
| CBT | 35% | 48% | 17% |
| GET | 28% | 17% | 54% |
| Pacing | 82% | 14% | 4% |

**Authors' summary:** CBT helped a small share of respondents (8–35%), GET brought "large negative responses" (54–74%), and pacing had the highest reported benefit (44–82%) and the lowest negative response rate. The authors note these findings conflict with RCTs, such as PACE, that reported CBT/GET to be effective and safe.

## Limitations
- **Bias:** the authors acknowledge sampling, response, and recall bias. Respondents were self-selected, mostly long-term ill, and recruited through a patient charity's survey. Many had taken overlapping treatments, and isolating single-treatment courses left small, likely underpowered groups.
- **Measurement:** outcomes are retrospective, self-rated, three-level change judgements. There are no objective measures, no control group, and no record of how each course was delivered.
- **Secondary surveys:** these were aggregated crudely, with overlapping respondents and differing questions.
- **Funding:** the data owner, a patient charity, gave a small post-publication award. The authors declare no conflicts.
- **Source used:** full text read from the authors' accepted post-print in the Coventry University repository. The published version is paywalled.
- **Corrections:** no retraction or correction is listed in Crossref or PubMed as of 2026-11-06.

## Abstract
Cognitive behavioural therapy and graded exercise therapy are promoted as evidence-based treatments for myalgic encephalomyelitis/chronic fatigue syndrome. This article explores patients' symptom responses following these treatments versus pacing therapy, an approach favoured by many sufferers. We analyse data from a large cross-sectional patient survey (n = 1428) and compare our findings with those from comparable patient surveys (n = 16,665), using a mix of descriptive statistics and regression analysis modelling. Findings from analysis of primary and secondary surveys suggest that cognitive behavioural therapy is of benefit to a small percentage of patients (8%-35%), graded exercise therapy brings about large negative responses in patients (54%-74%), while pacing is the most favoured treatment with the lowest negative response rate and the highest reported benefit (44%-82%). ^q1

## Quoted passages

### Methods
An online survey was opened for 4 months and advertised nationally to anyone with ME/CFS (not only members of the ME Association). Respondents were asked a series of 228 questions and sub-questions regarding treatment, particularly CBT, GET or PT. The survey had 1428 respondents. Respondents were required to confirm an affirmative diagnosis of ME/CFS from a qualified medical professional. Respondents also had to confirm that they had taken part in either one-to-one or group course therapy (CBT, GET, PT). Respondents had to indicate the severity of their illness and symptoms before and after their treatment course. Of the 1428 respondents, only 954 indicated that they had a confirmed medical diagnosis of ME/CFS, had participated in at least one of the three courses of treatment and completed questions on their symptoms and illness severity status, pre- and post-treatment (our main analysis cohort). ^q2

### Primary survey results
302 patients (32%) reported a worsening of their symptoms post-therapy, 455 (49%) reported that they stayed the same and 176 (19%) improved. When considering the main course content (ignoring other elements), Pacing Therapy (PT) brought about a greatest improvement rate in symptoms (44%) versus just 8% in CBT and 12% in GET. GET recorded the largest negative response (74% of patients) versus 18% in CBT and 14% in PT. ^q3

### Secondary surveys and caveat
Our search revealed more than 15 relevant surveys. We excluded 5 due to lack of clarity regarding treatment approach, leaving 10 included comparable surveys. CBT brings about improvement in symptoms for approximately 35% of respondents (65% unchanged/worse). 25% of GET reported improvement in symptoms (17% unchanged/54% worse), while 82% report benefit following PT with only 4% deterioration. ^q5

Much more detailed empirical research is needed to qualify these findings. ^q6
