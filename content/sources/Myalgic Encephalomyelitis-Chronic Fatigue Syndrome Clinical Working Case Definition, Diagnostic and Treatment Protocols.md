## Summary
This is the original Canadian Consensus Criteria (CCC) paper. Carruthers and 11 co-authors, an Expert Medical Consensus Panel convened under Health Canada terms of reference, published it in the Journal of Chronic Fatigue Syndrome 11(1):7–115. It gives a clinical working case definition built on patterns of symptom clusters, along with diagnostic and treatment protocols. It was intended for treating clinicians rather than for research selection. The panel argued that the CDC 1994 definition, which made severe fatigue the only compulsory criterion, made it harder to tell ME/CFS apart from ordinary fatigue or other fatiguing illnesses.

**This is a case definition, not a cohort.** The paper creates the Canadian Consensus Criteria (CCC), which require post-exertional malaise and/or fatigue with a pathologically slow recovery.

## Methods
- **Panel.** An Expert Subcommittee of Health Canada set the terms of reference and selected a panel of treating physicians, teaching faculty, and researchers.
- **Process.** A consensus workshop, held 30 March to 1 April 2001, concluded the review process. The panel reports collective experience diagnosing or treating more than 20,000 ME/CFS patients.
- **Indexing.** The DOI resolves through Crossref to Journal of Chronic Fatigue Syndrome 11(1):7–115 (January 2003; Haworth Press, now Taylor & Francis). The paper is not indexed in PubMed: searches of the title and of Carruthers BM with 2003 returned nothing.
- **Copy reviewed.** The definition and diagnostic-protocol pages were read from the copy linked by the US ME/CFS Clinician Coalition handout (phoenixrising.me, `Canadian-definition.pdf`, journal pages 7–28). There is no patient sample.

## Findings
- **Definition.** A patient must meet all of these:
  1. **Fatigue:** significant new-onset, unexplained, persistent or recurrent physical and mental fatigue that substantially reduces activity.
  2. **Post-exertional malaise and/or fatigue:** loss of stamina, rapid muscular and cognitive fatigability, and worsening of other symptoms, with a pathologically slow recovery, usually 24 hours or longer.
  3. **Sleep dysfunction.**
  4. **Pain:** myalgia, joint pain, or new headaches.
  5. **Neurological or cognitive:** two or more manifestations, such as confusion, impaired concentration and short-term memory, word-retrieval problems, perceptual disturbances, ataxia, or overload phenomena.
  6. **Autonomic, neuroendocrine, or immune:** at least one symptom from two of these three categories. Examples are orthostatic intolerance (NMH or POTS), IBS, and palpitations; thermostatic instability and heat or cold intolerance; and tender lymph nodes, recurrent sore throat, or new sensitivities.
  7. **Duration:** at least 6 months in adults and 3 months in children, with a preliminary diagnosis possible earlier.
- **Rare cases.** A small number of patients have no pain or sleep dysfunction. ME/CFS can still be considered when the onset was like an infectious illness.
- **Exclusions.** Active disease processes that explain most of the major symptoms are excluded. Named examples include Addison's disease, Cushing's syndrome, thyroid disease, anemias, iron overload, diabetes, cancer, treatable sleep disorders, rheumatological and neurological diseases, chronic infections, primary psychiatric disorders, and substance abuse. A condition that is under control does not exclude the diagnosis.
- **Comorbidities.** Fibromyalgia, IBS, migraine, depression, multiple chemical sensitivities, and others may coexist. ME/CFS and fibromyalgia are described as overlap syndromes.
- **Idiopathic chronic fatigue.** Unexplained prolonged fatigue with too few symptoms to meet the criteria is classed as idiopathic chronic fatigue.
- **Applying the definition.** The paper asks clinicians to assess total illness burden and the coherence of symptoms. A symptom counts as significant at roughly a 50% impact on life activities. Symptom severity hierarchy should be re-ranked periodically, and secondary symptoms and aggravators separated from primary ones.
- **Diagnostic protocol.** It covers a focused history and system review, a physical examination (including fibromyalgia tender points, Romberg, tandem gait, and lying and standing blood pressure), routine labs, and optional further tests: tilt table, sleep study, Holter monitoring, neuropsychological testing, and imaging.
- **Exercise and psychiatric overlap.** The paper contrasts ME/CFS with fibromyalgia on exercise response: aerobic exercise "often aggravates" ME/CFS. It distinguishes ME/CFS from primary depression by symptom pattern and timing.

## Limitations
- The paper's treatment-protocol section was not in the accessible copy, and the full journal version is paywalled, so its treatment protocols aren't summarized here.
- The IOM 2015 report observes that under the CCC a symptom only needs to be present to count, with no minimum frequency or severity. The 2010 Revised CCC by Jason et al. added those thresholds.
- This is a consensus definition; the panel cites but did not run validation studies.
- No retraction or correction notice was found in Crossref.

## Abstract
From the paper: an Expert Subcommittee of Health Canada established the terms of reference and selected an Expert Medical Consensus Panel of treating physicians, teaching faculty, and researchers. A consensus workshop held March 30 to April 1, 2001 concluded the review process and established consensus for a clinical working case definition, diagnostic protocols, and treatment protocols. The authors present a systematic clinical working case definition that encourages diagnosis based on characteristic patterns of symptom clusters reflecting specific areas of pathogenesis, along with diagnostic and treatment protocols and a short research overview. ^q1

## Quoted passages

### Clinical Working Case Definition of ME/CFS
A patient with ME/CFS will meet the criteria for fatigue, post-exertional malaise and/or fatigue, sleep dysfunction, and pain; have two or more neurological/cognitive manifestations and one or more symptoms from two of the categories of autonomic, neuroendocrine and immune manifestations; and adhere to item 7. ^q2

1. Fatigue: The patient must have a significant degree of new onset, unexplained, persistent, or recurrent physical and mental fatigue that substantially reduces activity level. 2. Post-Exertional Malaise and/or Fatigue: There is an inappropriate loss of physical and mental stamina, rapid muscular and cognitive fatigability, post exertional malaise and/or fatigue and/or pain and a tendency for other associated symptoms within the patient’s cluster of symptoms to worsen. There is a pathologically slow recovery period usually 24 hours or longer. ^q3

3. Sleep Dysfunction: There is unrefreshed sleep or sleep quantity or rhythm disturbances such as reversed or chaotic diurnal sleep rhythms. 4. Pain: There is a significant degree of myalgia. Pain can be experienced in the muscles and/or joints, and is often widespread and migratory in nature. Often there are significant headaches of new type, pattern or severity. ^q4

5. Neurological/Cognitive Manifestations: Two or more of the following difficulties should be present: confusion, impairment of concentration and short-term memory consolidation, disorientation, difficulty with information processing, categorizing and word retrieval, and perceptual and sensory disturbances (e.g., spatial instability and disorientation and inability to focus vision). Ataxia, muscle weakness and fasciculations are common. There may be overload phenomena: cognitive, sensory (e.g., photophobia and hypersensitivity to noise) and/or emotional overload, which may lead to crash periods and/or anxiety. ^q5

6. At Least One Symptom from Two of the Following Categories: Autonomic Manifestations: orthostatic intolerance—neurally mediated hypotension (NMH), postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome (POTS), delayed postural hypotension; light-headedness; extreme pallor; nausea and irritable bowel syndrome; urinary frequency and bladder dysfunction; palpitations with or without cardiac arrhythmias; exertional dyspnea. Neuroendocrine Manifestations: loss of thermostatic stability (subnormal body temperature and marked diurnal fluctuation, sweating episodes, recurrent feelings of feverishness and cold extremities); intolerance of extremes of heat and cold; marked weight change (anorexia or abnormal appetite); loss of adaptability and worsening of symptoms with stress. Immune Manifestations: tender lymph nodes, recurrent sore throat, recurrent flu-like symptoms, general malaise, new sensitivities to food, medications and/or chemicals. ^q6

7. The illness persists for at least six months. It usually has a distinct onset, although it may be gradual. Preliminary diagnosis may be possible earlier. Three months is appropriate for children. To be included, the symptoms must have begun or have been significantly altered after the onset of this illness. It is unlikely that a patient will suffer from all symptoms in criteria 5 and 6. The disturbances tend to form symptom clusters that may fluctuate and change over time. Children often have numerous prominent symptoms but their order of severity tends to vary from day to day. ^q7
